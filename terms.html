Layer 06 · The fine print

The Terms Table

Three questions decide a distributor (the service that puts your song on Spotify, Apple Music and the rest), and the yearly price is not one of them. What does it really cost once the money reaches an Australian bank account? What is the commission behind the "keep 100%" claim? And what happens to your catalogue the day you stop paying?

Twenty services, read from their own pricing pages and terms in the last week of August 2026. Prices converted at A$1 = US$0.7162, 30 August 2026. Where something could not be confirmed from a company's own pages it wears a reported tag. Corrections are welcome and get published.

Start here

Four ways to be charged.

Almost every service says you keep 100% of your royalties. Most of them are telling the truth about one line of income and staying quiet about the others. The model matters more than the price.

SubscriptionA yearly fee, no cut of streaming. The money comes from the fee, and from add-ons sold per release.
CommissionNo fee, or a small one, and a permanent percentage of everything the music earns.
Per releaseA one-off charge for each single or album, sometimes with a yearly renewal attached.
Free, at a priceNothing upfront, a larger percentage taken, or a tier that no longer delivers your music at all.

The question that matters

Four things can happen to your catalogue.

This is the field with the widest spread, and the one nobody puts on the pricing page. Every one of these is a choice the company made, because their competitors made a different one.

It stays up, free

Amuse and iMusician leave your releases live whether or not you keep paying, and Amuse advertises it: "no extra payments to keep your music available to the world, even if you cancel."

It stays up, for a cut

LANDR keeps it live and starts taking 15%. Too Lost is reported to do the same. The music survives; a commission arrives that was not there while you were paying.

It stays up, for a fee

EmuBands converts releases to permanent for a one-off charge. DistroKid sells the same thing per release: their help pages state it "is not a one-time payment for all of your releases". Twenty singles is about A$810.

It comes down

DistroKid without that purchase, UnitedMasters (which then deletes the data after a year), SoundCloud after 30 days. Your release date, your play counts and your playlist history go with it.

Keeping a file on a server and leaving a link alive costs a company close to nothing, which is why several of them do it for free and say so in their marketing. Taking the music down is not a technical limit. It is what makes the renewal notice work.

The money leaves in more places than one

What the price does not include.

Every figure below is from a company's own terms or fee page. None of them appear next to the price.

The withdrawal fee

DistroKid's minimum payout is US$6.00 and a bank transfer outside the United States costs US$5.62. Withdraw the minimum and 93 cents in every dollar goes to the fee. You need about A$390 sitting in the account before that fee drops below 2%. LANDR's international transfer is US$7.74, and an international wire in US dollars is US$40.24. SoundCloud's is US$3.75 to Australia. iGroove will not pay out below 100 units of currency at all.

The exchange rate you never see

The Australian services publish the mechanism most plainly, so their words are worth quoting. Their terms convert your streaming income at whatever rate the company obtains, credit your account at the "Average Bank Retail Spot Rate", and then state that the company "may retain the Distributor's Margin" between the two, with the artist assigning any claim to it. The retail rate is the worse one. The gap is theirs, and its size is not published anywhere. DistroKid's fee page says a conversion charge of "up to 3%" may apply.

The interest on your money

The same Australian terms pool artists' income in an interest-bearing account and assign that interest to the company. Your royalties earn something between the platform paying and you withdrawing. It is not yours.

The tax you can avoid, and probably are not

United States withholding on music royalties runs at 30% by default and 5% for an Australian resident under the Australia-US tax treaty. The only thing between those two numbers is a W-8BEN form. On US$300 of royalties, that form is worth A$101.58. CD Baby requires the form before it will pay you at all; not every service is as insistent.

The dormancy clause

The Australian services charge an Account Inactivity Fee equal to the income they are holding, up to $99, if you do not log in for two years. Money you earned, forfeited for not visiting.

One number to carry with you

Selling one album directly to one person, at A$15 on a platform taking 15%, puts about A$12 in your hands. Earning that same A$12 from Spotify takes roughly 3,000 plays.

And a track that gets fewer than 1,000 plays in a year earns nothing at all from Spotify, because it never enters the royalty pool. The research group MIDiA found the independent artist share of the market fell in 2025, and put it down to exactly that threshold.

Distribution is worth paying for when people are listening at scale. Before then, the door you own is worth more than the shelf you rent. The maker's path starts there for that reason.

Two facts to weigh alongside the table. G.Y.R.O. and Musible, the two services that charge in Australian dollars, both refuse music that is wholly or partly made with AI, so the cheapest local option is closed to a lot of people making music today. And APRA AMCOS, the body that collects songwriter royalties, publishes a warning worth repeating: be cautious if an aggregator asks you to waive performance or communication rights.
